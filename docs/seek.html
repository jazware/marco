seek: actor typeahead for atproto

A design, measured on a prototype. One prefix index over every actor's handle and display name. A signed-in viewer sees the people they follow first (from marco), everyone else ranks by PageRank over the follow graph, which the service recomputes daily. The numbers come from the whole network: 46.9M actor ids and 3.03B follows, on a 32-thread box with 61 GiB of RAM, 2026-11-14.

1. What it answers

The client asks its AppView. The AppView asks seek for ranked DIDs, hydrates them and answers. seek never builds a profile view.

seek is a skeleton store, like the AppView's other dataplane calls: a query prefix, an optional viewer and a limit in, ranked DIDs with scores out. It answers the dataplane's own SearchActorsTypeahead message, so an AppView that has the reference implementation's v2 path calls it with no new client code. The AppView keeps what it does today after the skeleton: hydration, takedowns, labels.

A client needs nothing new. Apps already call app.bsky.actor.searchActorsTypeahead on their AppView as the user types, with q and a small limit (the lexicon's default is 10, its max 100), signed in or not. What makes it feel instant is on the client: debounce, drop stale responses. seek's share of a keystroke is tens of microseconds, so the round trip is the AppView's.

3. The pieces

Solid arrows are live paths. Dashed ones run at bootstrap (and for repair).

seek holds three things in memory: the names (each actor's handle and display name), the prefix index built from them (a base rebuilt daily plus a small overlay of changes since), and one u16 rank per actor. marco answers the graph questions. PageRank runs next to marco on a checkpoint snapshot and hands seek a ranks file. Nothing in seek's hot path touches disk.

The query goes through the same fold, so a match is a byte prefix of a key. Keys are capped at 32 bytes. A longer query matches on its first 32 and nothing is lost in practice. A sorted array does what a weighted FST does here. Front-coding gets most of an FST's prefix sharing on this key set, lookup is a binary search over block heads and one block's scan, and building it is a sort. What the FST's max-weight outputs buy Lucene (the top of a huge prefix without a scan) the top lists buy here, and they're small because few prefixes are heavy. The base never changes between rebuilds, so it's one allocation per part with no pointers.

Live changes go in an overlay: a small sorted map of the keys of every actor changed since the base was built, and a set of those actors' ids. A query reads both and skips base postings whose actor is in the set. A day of profile and handle changes is a few hundred thousand actors, a few megabytes. The daily rebuild folds it in.

Follows first is the lever. In the mention set, 68% of the people mentioned were already followed by the author. Signed in, the mentioned account is in the top 3 after three characters 62% of the time, against 21% logged out, and it takes 3.5 characters on average to get there instead of 5.3 (8+ counts as 9).

The prior barely moves real queries. PageRank and follower count are within a point of each other on both sets, logged in or out. On new follows (people you didn't follow yet, ranked with the graph from before) follower count is a hair ahead: 22.2% against 21.3% at three characters. The wall costs discovery a little: signed in, the new account is behind the viewer's own matching follows (20.2%). That's the case second degree is for.

Where PageRank earns its keep: follow-back accounts. Of the 10,000 most-followed accounts, 1,322 fall outside PageRank's top 100,000 (41 of the top 1,000 fall outside its top 10,000). I found them by sorting the most-followed 10,000 by how far PageRank drops them. The 25 that drop furthest look alike: a median of 23.7k followers and about as many follows (follow-back accounts: in a January profile export their median was 24.2k followers and 25.0k follows, two years old, 1.7k posts), and they fall from a median position of 9,692 by followers to 172,750 by PageRank. A follower count can't tell a follow-back follower from a reader. PageRank can, because a follower who follows 25,000 accounts passes each of them 1/25,000 of a vote. These accounts rarely show up as mention targets, which is why the averages don't move.

Capping the pass fixes the head and keeps the rest. With each edge's pass capped at 1,000 average ranks (section 8), only 68 sources change, the one- and two-letter results look like the follower ones (well-known accounts, not the accounts the official ones follow), the follow-back accounts stay demoted (1,181 of the top 10,000 by followers still fall outside the top 100,000, against 1,322 plain), and on both query sets it's level with or a hair ahead of either: 21.9% on mentions and 22.3% on new follows at three characters, logged out.

8. PageRank

Pull: each subject reads its followers' contributions. One CSR of in-edges, built once per run.

The model is the textbook one. Damping 0.85, a uniform teleport, dangling mass (the 5.5M ids that follow nobody) spread uniformly, self-follows dropped, duplicate follows of the same subject counted once. marco hides taken-down and deleted accounts, so their follows pass no rank. A pass is a gather over 3.03B edges from a CSR of in-edges: memory-bound random reads of one f32 per edge on 32 threads. Exporting the CSR from marco's in-sets takes 10 s.

Cells are passes · seconds, export included. Stop rule: L1 < 1e-5. It needs no reference run, and by then the top 100,000 are in the final order to a Kendall tau of at least 0.99995: a handful of adjacent swaps among accounts whose ranks differ by less than the u16 can show. "u16 no longer changes" isn't a usable rule: values sit on rounding edges, and even at 1e-9 a few dozen of the top 100,000 still flip by one step. A warm daily run stops after 15 passes.

Cheaper passes. Renumbering sources by out-degree (hottest first) and sorting each in-list cuts a pass from 0.87 s to 0.34 s on the same box minutes apart, for 2.5 s of renumbering: the hot contributions share cache lines. A warm daily run to the stop rule is then about 16 s. Gauss–Seidel (in-place updates, so a pass sees this pass's new ranks) typically needs about half the passes of the plain power method, but in parallel it's racy and run-to-run nondeterministic, for a few seconds a day. Not worth it. Pass times varied from 0.85 to 1.8 s across runs of the same code on the same box, so compare within a session. A GPU doesn't help: a pass is random 4-byte reads over 12 GB, bound by memory bandwidth, and the box's GPU is an iGPU on the same memory.

Warm start. Yesterday's ranks start 0.0058 away in L1 (a week's are 0.0083), against 1.31 from uniform. That skips the first ~20 passes, about half the run whatever the tolerance. New ids start at 1/N and the vector is renormalised.

Without the CSR. Iterating straight over marco's in-sets costs 2.1 s a pass (each one decodes every Elias-Fano list) and peaks at 10 GB, against 25 GB with the CSR (12 GB of u32 sources plus a transient copy while it's built). A warm daily run to the stop rule is then about 45 s. The prototype's out-log export (two passes, used only to cut the graph by time for these tests) peaks at 31 GB.

Schedule and place. Daily, warm from the previous day: well under a minute of compute on a checkpoint snapshot. marco already writes checkpoints, and marco-load pagerank opens one read-only, so the job needs the snapshot's files and ~10 GB (in-set mode) or ~25 GB (CSR) on any box with the room, never inside a serving process. A day moves 0.07% of the follows, so running more often buys nothing typeahead can see.

Output. The job writes a u16 per actor, log-scaled between the smallest and largest rank (94 MB in marco's id order, and the u16 step is a relative error of 0.013%). seek has its own ids, so what it ships is keyed by DID: did:plc packed to 15 bytes as marco packs it (the DID string for the 5% that aren't did:plc), then the u16, sorted by DID, about 0.8 GB. seek merges it into the next base build in one pass beside its names, also sorted by DID. The job also writes each actor's in-degree, which is the follower count and, diffed against the day before, the recency signal.

The hub leak. bsky.app holds 6.8% of all rank (about 34M followers, most from sign-up) and follows a handful of accounts, so each of them gets a slice no follower count would give: accounts with a few thousand followers sit in the global top 10, and the official and team accounts lead every one-letter prefix. A cap on what one follower passes along one edge (here 1,000 average ranks, the excess to the teleport) touches only 68 sources and costs nothing: 85 iterations to 1e-9, the same as plain. Its effect on queries is in section 7 as "PageRank, pass capped". Plain PageRank stays the prior. The cap is an open question below.

The prototype took handles from PLC's claims without checking them. A claim is just a string in the DID document, so anyone can claim a big name's domain. seek must index only a handle that resolves back to the DID, the way the AppView shows handle.invalid otherwise. Recommendation: seek, a new package and service that calls marco. The marco calls a keystroke needs are one Filter (the follows and mutuals are a per-session cache), and that round trip is a small part of the AppView's own. Keeping strings out of marco keeps its memory and its scope what they are. PageRank runs as marco-load pagerank, next to the graph it reads, and its only output is a file. The API is the dataplane's message either way, so if we ever fold seek into the AppView's dataplane, nothing a caller sees changes.

12. Open questions

  1. Strictly follows first? As asked, a followed match always outranks a stranger. On the mentions set that's right most of the time, but a 1-letter query fills the page with follows. The alternative is a large boost (say +3 nats) instead of a wall. I'd ship the wall and look at it.
  2. Cap the pass? Plain PageRank is the prior. Its one visible flaw here is the hub leak: bsky.app's ~34M sign-up followers flow to the handful of accounts it follows, so a one-letter query shows accounts with a few thousand followers. Capping one edge's pass at 1,000 average ranks touches 68 sources, costs nothing, keeps the follow-back demotion and is level or a little ahead on both query sets. It's a flag on the same run. I'd turn it on.
  3. Who gets indexed. The prototype indexes the 44.5M actors in marco's directory with a handle or name. PLC has ~99M DIDs, most of them never followed anyone or were followed by no one. Indexing them all costs ~2× the keys for accounts nobody searches for. I'd index marco's directory plus anyone with a profile record.
  4. Second degree by default? It's the signal that finds new people your friends know, and it's the costliest per keystroke. It wants a batch Intersect in marco first.
  5. Exact handle when blocked. The reference AppView keeps an exact handle match even when there's a block, so a user can find who they blocked. seek does the same. Keep it?
  6. Where seek runs. ~2–3 GB of RAM and a few cores serve every keystroke of the network. It can share a box with anything that isn't marco's memory budget. Which AppView calls it first?

13. Reproducing the numbers

The box: 16 cores (32 threads), 61 GiB of RAM, NVMe. The follow and block records came from the archive's export (14 minutes), built into a marco snapshot in 2.1 min (47.0 GB). Handles came from PLC's current state, display names from the latest profile record per repo, mentions from two days of post facets. The prototype is crates/seek-proto here and marco-load pagerank in marco. Raw results and the scripts that ran them are in internal/seek/, beside this page's generator.
